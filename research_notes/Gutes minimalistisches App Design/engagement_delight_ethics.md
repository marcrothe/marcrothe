# Sustainable Engagement, Delight and Collecting: Psychology, Design and Ethical Boundaries

Context: guidance for a minimalist iPhone app where runners collect digital recreations of their race bibs (favorites via a pull-up gesture with a holographic shimmer, achievements, photo scanning of bibs). Research date: 2026-10-09. Note on sourcing: several primary sites (blog.duolingo.com, deceptive.design) were unreachable from the research environment, so some claims rely on secondary write-ups; this is flagged where relevant.

## 1. Habit and engagement models (Hook, Fogg, SDT) and intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation

### Takeaway
Hook and Fogg models explain *how* behaviour gets triggered; self-determination theory (SDT) explains *what sustains it*. For a bib-collection app the durable driver is need satisfaction (competence, autonomy, relatedness), not engineered triggers. Expected, contingent, tangible rewards measurably undermine intrinsic interest, while informational positive feedback enhances it.

### Cited Findings
- **Fogg Behavior Model (B=MAP):** behaviour occurs when Motivation, Ability and a Prompt converge at the same moment; if one is missing the behaviour does not happen — [behaviormodel.org](https://behaviormodel.org/)
- Fogg renamed "Trigger" to "Prompt" (B=MAT → B=MAP) in *Tiny Habits* (2019); motivation fluctuates, so increasing ability (reducing friction) is the more reliable lever; three prompt types: Spark (low motivation), Facilitator (low ability), Signal (both high) — [Koji guide (secondary)](https://www.koji.so/docs/fogg-behavior-model-guide); [LogRocket](https://blog.logrocket.com/ux-design/fogg-behavior-model)
- **Hook Model (Nir Eyal, *Hooked*):** four-step loop Trigger → Action → Variable Reward → Investment. External triggers = notifications, icons; internal triggers = emotions (boredom, loneliness, anxiety, FOMO) the user learns to associate with the product — [Koji guide (secondary)](https://www.koji.so/docs/hooked-model-habit-forming-products); [dsebastien concept note](https://concepts.dsebastien.net/concept/hook-model/)
- Investment step relies on escalating commitment: the more time/effort/data users put in, the more they value the product. Products with "finite variability" see the power of variable rewards decline over time — [Koji guide (secondary)](https://www.koji.so/docs/hooked-model-habit-forming-products)
- Critique framing: Hooked has been criticised as a playbook to "zombify your customers" — [strategy+business](https://strategy-business.com/blog/Zombify-Your-Customers)
- **SDT (Ryan & Deci 2000, *American Psychologist* 55(1)):** three universal, innate psychological needs — autonomy, competence, relatedness — described as "essential nutrients" for well-being and self-initiated behaviour; threats, deadlines, pressured directives diminish intrinsic motivation by shifting perceived locus of causality outward — [Wikipedia: SDT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-determination_theory); [PsychologyNotesHQ (secondary)](https://www.psychologynoteshq.com/self-determination-theory/)
- **SDT applied to games (Przybylski, Rigby & Ryan 2010, *Review of General Psychology* 14(2):154–166):** both the appeal and the well-being effects of games derive from satisfying competence, autonomy and relatedness; a 2009 companion study found enjoyment, value and desire for future play robustly associated with autonomy and competence experience — [ACU Research Bank](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/865zw/a-motivational-model-of-video-game-engagement); [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/7228/)
- The PENS (Player Experience of Need Satisfaction) questionnaire is free for academic use, commercial use requires permission from the Center for SDT — [selfdeterminationtheory.org](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/player-experience-of-needs-satisfaction-pens/)
- **Overjustification, Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973):** 51 preschoolers who already liked drawing; only the *expected* reward group ("Good Player Award" certificate) showed a significant drop in later free-choice drawing; unexpected-reward and no-reward groups showed no significant change — [UMass ScholarWorks PDF](https://scholarworks.umass.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bbbf0f9d-d9fe-4325-994d-6648714fff80/content); [PsychologyNotesHQ (secondary)](https://www.psychologynoteshq.com/overjustification-effect/)
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) meta-analysis, *Psychological Bulletin*, 128 experiments:** engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28); all tangible and all expected rewards had significant negative effects; **verbal positive feedback enhanced** free-choice behaviour (d = +0.33) and self-reported interest (d = +0.31) — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 PDF (UW)](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf); [CU Leeds copy](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/deci%201999.pdf)
- The undermining effect remains contested: Cameron & Pierce (1994) argued negative effects are limited and avoidable; Cameron, Banko & Pierce (2001) titled their rejoinder "Pervasive negative effects of rewards on intrinsic motivation: The myth continues" (i.e., disputing that the effect is pervasive) — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001 reply (SDT org)](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf); a later meta-analysis summary notes researchers "have failed to reach a consensus" — [utupub.fi](https://utupub.fi/handle/10024/173853)

### Inferences
- For the bib app, achievements should be framed as **informational feedback about the user's own running history** ("Your 10th half marathon", "First race abroad"), which maps to the d = +0.33 verbal/informational feedback finding, rather than as expected contingent prizes ("Scan 5 bibs to unlock X"), which maps to the undermining conditions.
- Unexpected recognition (surprise badges for things the runner already did) aligns with the Lepper result that unexpected rewards did not reduce interest.
- Running itself is already intrinsically/identity motivated; the app should not try to become the reason people race. Fogg's "ability" lever is the key design job: make scanning a bib nearly effortless (one photo, auto-extracted race/date/number).
- Hook's "internal triggers = negative emotions" component is the ethically riskiest part; an archival app has no need for it. Natural external triggers already exist (a race finish), so a prompt like "Add today's bib?" after a race date is a Fogg-style facilitator, not manipulation.

### Gaps
- Could not access primary texts of *Hooked* or *Tiny Habits*; model descriptions come from secondary summaries.
- No study found specifically measuring overjustification in fitness/collection apps.

## 2. Collection psychology (completion drive, endowed progress, IKEA, Zeigarnik, goal gradient, ownership)

### Takeaway
Head starts and visible progress toward a goal reliably increase persistence (endowed progress, goal gradient), effort raises valuation only when it ends in successful completion (IKEA), and ownership inflates valuation (endowment/mere ownership). The Zeigarnik "memory for unfinished tasks" claim does not replicate; the related tendency to *resume* tasks (Ovsiankina) does. Bib collections are personal, open-ended sets, so completion mechanics must be used carefully.

### Cited Findings
- **Endowed progress (Nunes & Drèze 2006, *Journal of Consumer Research*):** car wash field experiment, 300 cards; 8-stamp empty card vs. 10-stamp card with 2 stamps pre-filled (both require 8 purchases). Redemption over ~9 months: 34% (pre-stamped) vs 19% (empty). Effort increases and inter-purchase time decreases as people approach the goal; persistence depends on *relative* progress, not reward size; effect stronger when a reason for the endowment is given — [Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=5630); [Coglode summary](https://coglode.com/nuggets/endowed-progress-effect)
- Caution: the widely repeated "82% better" figure does not match 34% vs 19% (≈79% relative uplift) — [search-result analysis of Coglode/Wharton figures](https://coglode.com/nuggets/endowed-progress-effect)
- **Goal gradient (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006, *Journal of Marketing Research* 43:39–58):** café "buy 10 get 1 free" members bought coffee more frequently as they neared the reward; a 12-stamp card with 2 bonus stamps was completed faster than a plain 10-stamp card ("illusionary goal progress"); song-rating site users visited more often and rated more songs near the reward; stronger acceleration predicted greater retention and faster re-engagement; effort is a function of the *proportion* of distance remaining — [Columbia Business School PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf); [Columbia summary](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal)
- **IKEA effect (Norton, Mochon & Ariely 2012, *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 22(3):453–460):** participants valued self-assembled IKEA boxes, origami and Lego more; saw their amateur creations as similar in value to experts' and expected others to agree; effect held even for people uninterested in DIY; **labour increases valuation only when it results in successful completion** — when creations were destroyed or left incomplete, the effect dissipated — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1777100); [Harvard DASH PDF](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/12136084/norton%20mochon%20ariely%20third%20round[1].pdf)
- The "63% more willingness to pay" figure circulates in secondary sources but could not be verified against the paper — [Wikipedia: IKEA effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IKEA_effect)
- **Zeigarnik effect does not hold up:** Ghibellini & Meier (2025, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* 12, DOI 10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w) meta-analysis concludes the Ovsiankina effect (tendency to *resume* interrupted tasks) is general, whereas the Zeigarnik effect (better *memory* for interrupted tasks) "lacks universal validity"; secondary coverage reports a recall ratio of ~0.99 across studies excluding Zeigarnik's own — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html); [Univ. Bern PDF](https://www.kog.psy.unibe.ch/e48276/e1785865/s41599-025-05000-w1_ger.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect) (exact study counts conflict between secondary sources)
- **Endowment effect (Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler 1990, *J. Political Economy* 98(6)):** randomly endowed mug owners demanded ~$5.25 to sell vs non-owners willing to pay ~$2.25–2.75; trade volume far below the ~50% predicted by Coase theorem — [MIT-hosted PDF](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_et_al_1990_Experimental_tests.pdf); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/v98y1990i6p1325-48.html)
- **Mere ownership (Beggan 1992, *JPSP* 62(2):229–237):** owners rate an object more favourably than non-owners, explained via self-enhancement (possessions linked to self); mechanism later challenged (dissonance account, preference confound) and replications only partially supportive — [Wikipedia: Mere ownership effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mere_ownership_effect)
- **Why people collect:** collecting is hedonic, highly involving consumption forming a set of items removed from ordinary use (Belk et al. 1991); collectors' motives are combinations of financial and hedonic drivers (study of 208 collectors) — [Saridakis et al., White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/125015/2/EJM-Saridakis.pdf)
- Set completion is a distinct motive from financial gain; as a set takes shape, single pieces are valued for what they add to the whole (Carey, *J. Economic Psychology*, via summary); many collectors pursue completion for internal achievement — [Psychology Today, "Collecting With Purpose"](https://www.psychologytoday.com/nz/blog/the-mind-of-a-collector/202404/collecting-with-purpose-completing-a-set)
- **Desire for control** motivates collecting (2025 *JCR*, "Seeking Structure in Collections"); the motivating effect diminishes when structure-seeking is hindered or when the collection is far from completion — [Arizona experts page](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/seeking-structure-in-collections-desire-for-control-motivates-eng/); [Lingnan scholars page](https://scholars.ln.edu.hk/en/publications/seeking-structure-in-collections-desire-for-control-motivates-eng/)
- Belk's extended self: possessions function as extensions of the self; losing them can feel like losing personal history — [Merca2.0 (secondary)](https://www.merca20.com/psychologists-agree-collecting-is-not-an-obsession-but-a-behavior-with-proven-psychological-benefits-for-people/)

### Inferences
- A bib collection is a self-defined, memory-laden set (extended self), not a predefined checklist. Structure and control matter: let users organise by year, distance, city, "favourites" — the 2025 JCR finding suggests structure itself motivates.
- Use goal gradient/endowed progress only on **genuine, user-meaningful sets** (e.g., "World Marathon Majors 3/7", "All distances: 5K, 10K, HM, M") where the progress is real (they already ran races that count). Pre-filling with fake progress (the Nunes-Drèze trick) would feel hollow and borderline deceptive in a personal archive.
- Photo-scanning plus light confirming/correcting of extracted data is a small, successfully completed effort → IKEA-style attachment; but abandonments (failed scans) negate it, so scan reliability is central (consistent with Walter's hierarchy, section 4).
- Don't design around "Zeigarnik" open loops; the evidence supports a resumption tendency at most, which justifies gently showing an unfinished draft bib, not nagging.
- The holographic "favourite" gesture leverages ownership/specialness; scarcity of favourites (e.g., limited slots) could strengthen meaning but should be user-adjustable to avoid artificial constraint.

### Gaps
- No studies found on digital-only collections' endowment effect specifically (vs physical objects); the 63% IKEA WTP figure unverified; Carey and Belk primary texts not accessed.

## 3. Variable rewards, manipulation, dark patterns and humane technology principles

### Takeaway
Variable rewards are powerful because unpredictability sustains anticipation, but they become manipulative when tied to money, loss aversion or compulsion (loot boxes, punitive streaks). Regulators (FTC 2022 staff report, EU DSA Art. 25) define dark patterns by effect — impairing autonomous, informed choice — not by intent. Streaks increase behaviour but broken streaks demotivate, and paid streak repair is a commercial lever.

### Cited Findings
- **Variable reward mechanism (Hook model):** reward is variable and unpredictable, which keeps users curious about what they might receive next — [Koji guide (secondary)](https://www.koji.so/docs/hooked-model-habit-forming-products)
- **Loot boxes:** Zendle & Cairns (2018, *PLoS ONE*, n = 7,422) found loot box spending associated with problem gambling severity (η² = 0.054), far stronger than other in-game spending (η² = 0.004); 2019 replication (n = 1,172, blinded) η² = 0.051; 2020 study (*Computers in Human Behavior*): link holds regardless of cash-out or pay-to-win features. Correlational; direction (gateway vs attraction) unresolved — [White Rose eprint 139116](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/139116); [White Rose eprint 143229](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/143229); [White Rose eprint 148267](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/148267/)
- **Streaks evidence (Silverman, Barasch, Inman, Wood & Lee 2023, *JCR*, "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions", DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucac029):** displaying a streak increases persistence; a broken streak lowers subsequent engagement, more so when the break is highlighted; app-caused breaks less demotivating than self-caused — [Olson Zaltman summary](https://www.olsonzaltman.com/zfiles/freaky-streaks); paid repair options can weaken motivation to maintain the streak — [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification)
- **Duolingo (industry data, self-reported):** >600 experiments on streaks over four years (Jackson Shuttleworth, Lenny's Podcast summary) — [Recall summary](https://www.recall.it/summary/lennys-podcast/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks-or-jackson-shuttleworth-group-pm-retention-team); reported A/B results: always-visible streak counter +3% DAU, +1% D14 retention; streak wager +5% D14 and +600% IAP revenue; streak freezes purchasable with virtual currency — [Econsultancy](https://econsultancy.com/six-a-b-tests-used-by-duolingo-to-tap-into-habit-forming-behaviour/). Duolingo's own blog notes diminishing motivational lift per extra streak day — [Duolingo blog: streak research](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-streak-research/) (could not be fetched directly; via search summary)
- Streak "anxiety/shame/burnout" claims come mainly from practitioners (e.g., Yu-kai Chou's opinion that problems emerge past ~30 days), not controlled studies — [Yu-kai Chou](https://yukaichou.com/?p=38981); a frequently cited "68% of streak breakers report anxiety" statistic could not be traced to a real study.
- **FTC staff report "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" (15 Sep 2022)**, following an April 2021 workshop: dark patterns = "design practices that trick or manipulate users into making choices they would not otherwise have made and that may cause harm"; covers e-commerce, cookie banners, children's apps, subscriptions; applies across websites, mobile apps, VR/AR; categories include inducing false beliefs (fake scarcity, countdown timers, disguised ads) and unauthorized charges/hard-to-cancel subscriptions; also hiding material information and obscuring privacy choices — [Hunton Andrews Kurth](https://www.huntonprivacyblog.com/2022/09/27/ftc-releases-report-on-dark-patterns/); [Orrick InfoBytes](https://infobytes.orrick.com/2022-09-23/ftc-reports-use-dark-patterns/); [ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/ftc-state-ag/ftc-report-signals-doubling-down-on-enforcement-against-dark-patterns/)
- **EU DSA Art. 25(1):** online platforms shall not "design, organise or operate their online interfaces in a way that deceives or manipulates" users or materially distorts/impairs their ability to make free and informed decisions; Art. 25(2) lists for Commission guidance: (a) giving more prominence to certain choices, (b) repeatedly requesting a choice already made, (c) making termination harder than subscribing — [Springlex DSA Art. 25 (OJ L 277)](https://www.springlex.eu/en/packages/dsa/dsa-regulation/article-25/)
- **DSA Recital 67:** dark patterns = practices that "materially distort or impair, either on purpose or in effect" users' ability to make autonomous, informed choices; examples include hard-to-change defaults and making cancellation unreasonably hard; legitimate, law-compliant practices are not per se dark patterns — [Prighter: Recital 67](https://prighter.com/resources/laws/dsa/recitals/recital-67)
- Art. 25 applies only to online platforms; UCPD and GDPR overlap for other services; the EU's planned Digital Fairness Act targets dark patterns more broadly — [Maastricht University blog](https://www.maastrichtuniversity.nl/blog/2023/04/destination-%E2%80%98dark-patterns%E2%80%99-eu-digital-legislative-train-and-line-drawing); [Osborne Clarke: Digital Fairness Act](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/digital-fairness-act-unpacked-dark-patterns)
- **Brignull taxonomy:** Brignull coined "dark pattern" in 2010 and now prefers "deceptive pattern"; deceptive.design lists ~16 types incl. Confirmshaming, Fake Scarcity, Fake Urgency, Hard to Cancel, Disguised Ads, Hidden Costs, Forced Continuity, Sneaking, Nagging, Trick Questions, Privacy Zuckering, Roach Motel — [deceptive.design book ch. 13 (Mathur et al. taxonomy)](https://deceptive.design/book/contents/chapter-13); [UW CSE340 slides](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse340/22sp/slides/wk09/Dark-Patterns-Deceptive-Design.pdf); [NN/g: Deceptive patterns](https://nngroup.com/articles/deceptive-patterns/)
- **Gray et al. (2018) five strategies:** Nagging, Obstruction, Sneaking, Interface Interference, Forced Action — [UW CSE340 slides](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse340/22sp/slides/wk09/Dark-Patterns-Deceptive-Design.pdf)
- **Center for Humane Technology:** "Seven Principles of Humane Technology" (Raskin & Fernando); design is never value-neutral, values should be explicit guardrails; aim to avoid manipulative tactics exploiting attention/emotion and to support well-being; Humane Tech Design Guide via the Foundations in Humane Technology course — [CHT: What do we mean by humane tech?](https://centerforhumanetechnology.substack.com/p/what-do-we-mean-by-human-tech); [humanetech.com: humane product design](https://www.humanetech.com/humane-product-design)
- German consumer federation vzbv (2023) assessed Amazon, Booking.com, Google Shopping, YouTube as wholly or partly non-compliant with the DSA dark pattern ban (advocacy assessment, not enforcement) — [vzbv PDF](https://www.vzbv.de/sites/default/files/2023-12/vzbv_Evaluation_DSA_bf.jpeg_.pdf)

### Inferences
- A legitimate place for variability in the bib app: **content-based variability** (each bib looks different; shimmer reacts differently to tilt; occasional surprise milestone recognition) rather than **reward-schedule variability** (random drops, gacha-like unlocks). Never sell random rewards.
- Running has natural gaps (race calendars are seasonal; injuries), so daily streaks are a mismatch and would produce exactly the broken-streak demotivation Silverman et al. describe. If any continuity metric is used, make it yearly ("races per year") and non-punitive.
- Apply DSA/FTC tests as internal heuristics even if the app isn't a "platform": no confirmshaming on un-favouriting, no nagging re-prompts for notifications/ratings, cancelling any subscription as easy as subscribing, no fake scarcity on premium bib skins.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch deceptive.design's current type list or the FTC PDF directly; Commission guidelines on Art. 25 and current status of the Digital Fairness Act proposal (as of Oct 2026) not verified.
- Spicer et al. (2022) loot box meta-analysis mentioned in search results was not verified.
- No primary source located for the full text of CHT's seven principles.

## 4. Microinteractions and delight (Saffer, Walter, Disney principles, iOS haptics, celebrations)

### Takeaway
Delight sits on top of function, reliability and usability; it cannot compensate for failures below. Microinteractions should be designed as trigger → rules → feedback → loops/modes, with feedback (visual, motion, haptic) tightly bound to cause. Frequency is the main determinant of whether an animation delights or annoys: reserve rich motion and celebration for rare moments.

### Cited Findings
- **Saffer, *Microinteractions: Designing with Details* (O'Reilly 2013):** four parts — Trigger (manual or system), Rules (sequence, what can/can't be done), Feedback (graphics, sounds, vibrations; rules are invisible so feedback is how users understand them; absence of feedback is also information), Loops & Modes (meta-rules: repetition/change over time, user-set modes) — [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/microinteractions/9781449342760/); [uxdesign.cc](https://uxdesign.cc/think-smaller-engaging-users-through-microinteractions-9f10372f2e1b); [i-programmer review](https://www.i-programmer.info/bookreviews/159-uiux/7510-microinteractions.html)
- **Walter, *Designing for Emotion* (A Book Apart):** Maslow-style hierarchy of user needs — Functional → Reliable → Usable → Pleasurable; upper levels depend on lower ones; a beautiful product that doesn't solve the problem fails — [Mozilla UX blog](https://blog.mozilla.org/ux/2011/10/why-not-add-pleasurability-testing-into-our-usability-tests/); [Ben Nadel review](https://www.bennadel.com/blog/2293-a-book-apart-designing-for-emotion-by-aarron-walter.htm)
- Walter's argument draws on the aesthetic-usability finding: attractive products are perceived as more usable and users tolerate minor usability issues; Mozilla proposed adding pleasurability questions to usability tests — [Mozilla UX blog](https://blog.mozilla.org/ux/2011/10/why-not-add-pleasurability-testing-into-our-usability-tests/)
- **Disney's 12 principles in UI:** squash & stretch scaled to the simulated material (rubber vs bowling ball); short anticipation before main action; staging to direct attention to key elements; slow-in/slow-out (easing); restrained exaggeration reads as responsive rather than theatrical; no measured usability evidence for these mappings — [IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/ui-animation-how-to-apply-disney-s-12-principles-of-animation-to-ui-design); [uxdesign.cc](https://uxdesign.cc/disneys-12-principles-of-animation-exemplified-in-ux-design-5cc7e3dc3f75); [Dribbble](https://dribbble.com/stories/2020/07/27/disney-principles-of-animation-ui-interactions)
- **Timing:** NN/g: animations can make UIs easier and nicer, but timing must be right — [NN/g UX Animations video](https://nngroup.com/videos/ux-animations); 100 ms perceived as instant, ~1 s upper limit for flow of thought; simple feedback animations ~100 ms; most UI animation 200–500 ms — [Val Head](https://valhead.com/?p=2978); [W3C CSS archive citing NN/g](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-css-archive/2025Apr/0702.html)
- **Frequency rule:** an animation on a rarely used element is a pleasant surprise; the same on an element used many times a day becomes an annoyance; high-frequency actions may be best with no animation — [Emil Kowalski, "You don't need animations"](https://emilkowal.ski/ui/you-dont-need-animations); "delight budget" belongs to rare/first-time moments (onboarding, empty states, success, celebration) — [Lordicon blog](https://lordicon.com/blog/from-delight-to-annoyance-when-too-much-animation-hurts-ux)
- **Apple HIG, Playing haptics:** use system haptic patterns according to documented meanings; build a clear causal relationship between each haptic and its action ("a haptic that doesn't reinforce cause and effect can be confusing and seem gratuitous"); standard controls (switches, sliders, pickers) already play haptics — [Apple HIG: Playing haptics](https://developer-mdn.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/patterns/playing-haptics)
- Apple developer guidance: "Don't overuse feedback. Overuse can cause confusion and diminish the feedback's significance"; feedback must match a visual change or respond to a user action and "should never come as a surprise"; don't choose a haptic for how it feels — [Apple: Playing haptic feedback in your app](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/documentation/applepencil/playing-haptic-feedback-in-your-app.md); Core Haptics introduction — [WWDC19 session 520](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/520)

### Inferences
- **Pull-up-to-favourite gesture** maps cleanly onto Saffer: trigger = vertical drag on a bib; rules = threshold distance commits favourite, release before threshold cancels; feedback = progressive shimmer intensity tracking finger, a single haptic "detent" at commit threshold, settle animation with slight overshoot (squash/stretch scaled for "card stock" material = subtle); loops/modes = shimmer subtly re-appears on device tilt in the favourites view only.
- The holographic shimmer is the app's signature delight; keep it **tied to user input** (tilt, drag) rather than ambient looping, which respects Apple's causality rule and avoids novelty decay.
- Celebration budget: full celebration (e.g., confetti or a bespoke hologram burst) only for rare events — first bib, a new distance, a marathon, a year review — never for every scan. Routine scan success should be a quick, quiet confirmation (~200–300 ms + light/success haptic).
- Walter's hierarchy implies the OCR/scanning reliability and data correctness are prerequisites; delight on a failed scan amplifies frustration.
- Respect Reduce Motion and make haptics optional (implied by Apple accessibility guidance; verify current HIG wording).

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on confetti/celebration frequency and user annoyance or novelty decay rates in apps; guidance is practitioner-based.
- Exact NN/g article on animation duration not fetched directly; figures via secondary citations.

## 5. Meaningful value over time (memories, identity, storytelling, year in review)

### Takeaway
Annual retrospectives are among the most effective engagement and acquisition mechanics in consumer apps because they turn accumulated personal data into identity-affirming, shareable stories. A bib archive naturally accumulates personal history, so a runner-specific "year in bibs" is a strong, non-manipulative value moment.

### Cited Findings
- **Spotify Wrapped 2025:** 200 million engaged users in ~24 hours (+19% YoY; in 2024 it took ~62 hours to reach 200M); "engaged" = viewed at least one Wrapped story; >500 million shares (+41% YoY) including native shares, screenshots and downloads — [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/spotify-wrapped-campaign-hit-200m-engaged-users-in-24-hours-a-19-yoy-increase); [Music Week](https://www.musicweek.com/digital/read/spotify-wrapped-2025-was-biggest-ever-with-200-million-engaged-users/093178)
- Wrapped 2024 ultimately engaged 245 million users (Alex Norström); 2025 added identity-style features (Listening Age, "Clubs" of six listening styles, Wrapped Party) — [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/spotify-wrapped-campaign-hit-200m-engaged-users-in-24-hours-a-19-yoy-increase); [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91454685/spotify-wrapped-2025-eagerly-checking-your-listening-age-everyone-else-is-too)
- Spotify reached 751M MAU in Q4 2025 (+38M net adds), with Wrapped cited as a driver; Daniel Ek called Wrapped a "huge driver behind our MAU and subscriber growth" — [Hypebeast (IT), relaying Bloomberg](https://hypebeast.com/it/2026/2/spotify-wrapped-drives-record-751-million-monthly-users)
- **Strava Year in Sport 2025 (12th edition, released 3 Dec 2025):** based on billions of activities plus a survey of >30,000 people; Gen Z race participation up 28% (5K), 39% (10K), 31% (half), 33% (marathon) YoY; Gen Z 75% more likely than Gen X to cite a race/event as main exercise motivation; running remained most-recorded activity — [Athletech News](https://athletechnews.com/running-walking-strength-training-strava-year-in-sport-report/); [PR release via Webull](https://www.webull.com/news/13961203642123264); [Running Insight](https://www.runninginsight.com/movement-is-in-running-is-cool-and-community-rules)
- Belk's extended-self concept: possessions as extensions of the self and personal history — [Merca2.0 (secondary)](https://www.merca20.com/psychologists-agree-collecting-is-not-an-obsession-but-a-behavior-with-proven-psychological-benefits-for-people/)

### Inferences
- Races are explicitly identity- and event-driven for younger runners (Strava data), so bibs function as identity tokens; the app's long-term value is a **personal running memoir** (where, when, with whom, what time, how it felt), not points.
- A "Year in Bibs" recap (races, km raced, cities, PRs, the favourite of the year) offers a Wrapped-style shareable artifact; design it to be shareable as a beautiful image (Spotify counts screenshots as shares), and time it after the main autumn marathon season.
- "On this day" memory resurfacing (anniversary of a first marathon) provides a meaningful, low-frequency, non-coercive return trigger, consistent with SDT relatedness/competence.
- Allow notes/photos per bib to deepen storytelling (also an IKEA-style investment, but one that serves the user).

### Gaps
- No public retention data found specifically attributing long-term retention to Strava's Year in Sport; Spotify's full 2025 Wrapped total not officially reported; no published data on "On this day" features (e.g., Apple/Google Photos memories) retention effects found.

## 6. Measured effects summary (studies, retention data, case studies)

### Takeaway
Hard numbers exist for loyalty-card progress effects, reward undermining, loot box/gambling correlations and Wrapped reach; app-level retention evidence for streaks and gamification is mostly company-reported and unaudited.

### Cited Findings
- Endowed progress: 34% vs 19% completion — [Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=5630)
- Goal gradient: purchase acceleration near reward predicts retention — [Kivetz et al. 2006 PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf)
- Rewards undermining: d = −0.28 to −0.40 for contingent rewards; +0.33 for positive feedback — [Deci et al. 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)
- Loot boxes ↔ problem gambling: η² ≈ 0.05 (n = 7,422 and 1,172) — [White Rose 139116](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/139116)
- Duolingo streak tests: +3% DAU, +1% D14 (visible counter); +5% D14, +600% IAP (wager) — [Econsultancy](https://econsultancy.com/six-a-b-tests-used-by-duolingo-to-tap-into-habit-forming-behaviour/) (company-reported, no methodology)
- Spotify Wrapped 2025: 200M engaged users in 24h, 500M+ shares — [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/spotify-wrapped-campaign-hit-200m-engaged-users-in-24-hours-a-19-yoy-increase)
- Endowment: WTA ~$5.25 vs WTP ~$2.25–2.75 — [Kahneman et al. 1990 PDF](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_et_al_1990_Experimental_tests.pdf)

### Inferences
- The strongest evidence supports (a) real progress visualisation, (b) informational feedback over contingent prizes, (c) shareable annual retrospectives. The weakest/most ethically fraught evidence base is for streak pressure and random rewards; both are poor fits for a race-bib archive.
- Suggested internal success metrics aligned with humane design: scans completed per race run, % bibs with notes/photos, year-review views/shares, and self-reported "this app reminds me of good memories", rather than DAU or session length.

### Gaps
- No independent, peer-reviewed retention data found for gamified fitness collection apps; Duolingo figures cannot be verified; no measured data on holographic/shimmer effects or haptic delight on retention.
